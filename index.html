Genealogy · Population Genetics · Pedigree Collapse

You do not have
2n ancestors.

The viral argument counts ancestral slots in an Ahnentafel (ancestor table), then pretends every slot was a different person. In a finite — and especially in an endogamous (marrying-within-the-group) population — the same ancestors fill many slots. That is pedigree collapse. This lab lets you model it.

THE FALLACY   230 slots ≈ 1.07 billion people c. 1200 CE — more than lived on Earth
THE FIX   slots ≠ people. Count distinct ancestors \(A_g\), not slots \(S_g=2^g\).

Model controls

All population sizes below are effective breeding population size \(N_e\) — the size of an idealised random-mating population with the same rate of genetic drift — not census size \(N_c\). Typically \(N_e/N_c \approx 0.1\!-\!0.3\).

Pedigree collapse & multiplicity

Collapse \(C_g = 1-A_g/S_g\). Multiplicity \(m_g=S_g/A_g\) = average number of slots one distinct ancestor occupies — how many times they appear in your Ahnentafel.

Population trajectory \(N_e(g)\) & implied \(N_c(g)\)

Looking backwards: expansion unwinds, the founder bottleneck bites, then the older population. The solid \(N_e(g)\) curve — not a constant — drives everything else; dashed is the head-count \(N_c(g)=N_e/v(g)\) you'd need to produce it.

Inbreeding coefficient \(F_g\)

Plain language: pick one spot in a child's DNA. They got one copy from their mother, one from their father. \(F_g\) is the probability those two copies are actually copies of the same ancestral DNA — because the parents share ancestors (Identity by Descent, IBD). Equivalently, it's roughly the expected share of the child's genome sitting in runs of homozygosity (ROH). It is not a percentage of shared ancestors, and not the collapse percentage. The subscript \(g\): how much of this has accumulated after \(g\) generations of mating inside this population — deeper \(g\), smaller \(N_e(g)\), and more cousin unions all push it up, via \(\Delta F \approx 1/(2N_e)\) per generation (Wright's inbreeding coefficient).

The mathematics

$$ S_g = 2^{g} \qquad\text{(ancestral slots / Ahnentafel places at depth }g\text{)} $$

1 · Occupancy (birthday-problem) model. Treat the \(S_g\) slots as draws with replacement from \(N_e(g)\) potential ancestors. The expected number of distinct people is the classic occupancy result:

$$ \mathbb{E}[A_g] \;=\; N_e(g)\left[1-\left(1-\tfrac{1}{N_e(g)}\right)^{S_g}\right] \;\approx\; N_e(g)\left(1-e^{-S_g/N_e(g)}\right), \qquad C_g = 1-\frac{A_g}{S_g},\quad m_g=\frac{S_g}{A_g} $$

When \(S_g \ll N_e\), \(A_g\approx S_g\) (almost no collapse — the video's intuition works for ~10 generations). When \(S_g \gg N_e\), \(A_g\to N_e\): saturation. Crossover at \(g^*=\log_2 N_e\). The table's \(\sum_{k\le g} A_k\) column adds the per-generation distinct counts: since different generations are mostly different birth cohorts, that sum is close to the total number of distinct people in the whole pedigree up to depth \(g\) (it slightly overcounts anyone who appears at two different depths, which requires large parental-age differences).

2 · Endogamy as a shrunken mating pool. With endogamy rate \(e\) and subgroup share \(s\), mates come from an effective pool \(N_e^{\text{mate}} = N_e\big[(1-e)+e\,s\big]\). At \(e=0.9,\ s=0.15\) the pool is only \(0.235\,N_e\) — collapse arrives \(\log_2(1/0.235)\approx2.1\) generations sooner, every generation.

2b · Why \(N_e \ll N_c\), and why their quotient \(v=N_e/N_c\) is not a constant of nature. \(N_c\) counts heads: children, the elderly, the childless, everyone. \(N_e\) counts only the population's genetic contribution to the next generation, and three mechanisms shrink it. (i) Variance in reproductive success: if family size has variance \(V_k\) (mean \(\bar k=2\) in a stable population), \(N_e \approx (4N-2)/(V_k+2)\) — Poisson family sizes (\(V_k=2\)) give \(N_e\approx N\), but human \(V_k\) is larger (some lineages have many children, many have none; historically, high-status male skew could be extreme), so \(N_e\) falls. (ii) Unequal breeding sex ratio: \(N_e = 4N_mN_f/(N_m+N_f)\) — a small number of breeding males caps \(N_e\) near \(4N_m\) no matter how large \(N_f\) is. (iii) Fluctuating size: over \(t\) generations \(N_e\) is the harmonic mean, \(1/N_e = \tfrac1t\sum_i 1/N_i\), which is dominated by the smallest generation — one bottleneck generation at 350 outweighs a century at 100,000. Add overlapping generations, subdivision, and selection at linked sites, and long-term human \(N_e/N_c\) of roughly 0.1–0.3 is typical — but \(V_k\), sex ratio and size all changed across eras (famine, war, plague, polygyny, later fertility transition), so \(v\) changed too. Hence the controls: \(v_0\) today, \(v_{\text{past}}\) at the bottleneck, interpolated in between if you tick the box. \(v\) never enters the dynamics — drift, coalescence and collapse see only \(N_e\) — it is purely the bridge \(N_c=N_e/v\) between the model and historical head-counts, and the table now shows it generation by generation.

$$ N_e \approx \frac{4N-2}{V_k+2}, \qquad N_e = \frac{4N_mN_f}{N_m+N_f}, \qquad \frac{1}{N_e} = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{1}{N_i}, \qquad v(g)=\frac{N_e(g)}{N_c(g)} $$
$$ N_e^{\text{mate}}(g)=N_e(g)\big[(1-e)+e\,s\big],\qquad F_g = 1-\prod_{k=1}^{g}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2N_e^{\text{mate}}(k)}\right),\qquad \Delta F\approx \frac{1}{2N_e} $$

3 · Wright's path method & kinship. For a specific pedigree, \(F_X=\sum ( \tfrac12 )^{n+m+1}(1+F_A)\) summed over all paths through common ancestors \(A\) (\(n,m\) = generations from each parent to \(A\)). The coefficient of relationship \(r=2\phi\) (\(\phi\) = kinship/coancestry coefficient) gives additive genetic relatedness: parent–child \(r=\tfrac12\), first cousins \(r=\tfrac18\), and — the punchline — two random members of the same population share small but non-zero \(r\) through thousands of such paths. Offspring of first cousins: \(F=\tfrac1{16}\); second cousins: \(F=\tfrac1{64}\). This lab blends drift-\(F\) with a consanguinity term \(F \leftarrow 1-(1-F_{\text{drift}})(1-\alpha/16)\) as a crude mixture.

3b · Consanguineous unions (structural, on top of random collapse). Occupancy with \(N_e\) already captures chance relatedness. Systematic cousin marriage adds more: in a first-cousin union the parents share a pair of grandparents, so \(\tfrac14\) of all Ahnentafel slots above that union are duplicates (the shared pair's entire subtrees); for second cousins, \(\tfrac18\). If a fraction \(\alpha\) of unions at every ancestral generation are consanguineous, with mean duplicated fraction \(\bar d\approx0.20\) (60/40 first/second-cousin mix), distinct ancestors are thinned multiplicatively — overlaps-of-overlaps neglected, a first-order approximation:

$$ A_g^{(\text{cons})} = A_g^{(\text{rand})}\,\big(1-\alpha\bar d\big)^{\,g-1}, \qquad \bar d \approx 0.20 $$

Note the compounding in \(g\): at \(\alpha=0.05\) this is only a 1% thinning per generation, but \((0.99)^{19}\approx0.83\) by \(g=20\) and \((0.99)^{34}\approx0.71\) by \(g=35\) — deep in the pedigree, a large share of the distinct ancestors that random collapse alone would leave are gone. At \(\alpha=0\) the factor is exactly 1 and the model reduces to pure occupancy. This is why the \(\alpha\) slider barely matters at \(g\le10\) and matters a great deal deep in the pedigree — and why it also appears in \(F\).

4 · Coalescent & identical ancestors — two different "MRCAs", do not confuse them. (a) Genetic coalescence (Kingman): trace two gene copies at one locus backwards; they coalesce (were the same DNA molecule) with probability only \(\approx 1/(2N_e)\) per generation, so the expected waiting time is \(\approx 2N_e\) generations — yes, a lot: at long-term human \(N_e\approx10^4\) that's \(\approx\)20,000 generations, with a huge (geometric) variance, and it is exactly why many autosomal loci have common ancestors hundreds of thousands of years old, while the mitochondrial and Y-chromosome MRCAs are \(\sim\)150–300 thousand years. Note it is \(2\mathbf{N_e}\), never \(2N_c\): with a time-varying pool the rate accumulates as a hazard, \(\sum_g 1/(2N_e(g))\), so a bottleneck generation contributes as much coalescence as thousands of generations in a large population — lineages usually coalesce during the bottleneck, not after waiting \(2N_e\) of today's size. (b) Genealogical MRCA (Chang): the most recent person who is a pedigree ancestor of two (or all) present-day people needs no DNA transmission at all, and sits only \(\sim\!\log_2 N\) generations back; the identical ancestors point (IAP) — where everyone alive then is either ancestor of everyone today or of no one — is at \(\approx1.77\log_2 N\) (Chang 1999), and Rohde, Olson & Chang (2004) showed this survives realistic population structure. Structure and endogamy push (b) back, but not to infinity. Pedigree collapse is about (b); inbreeding and IBD are about (a). That distinction, not \(2^g\), is the correct picture.

What this model is not. It assumes random mating within the (shrunken) pool, discrete generations, and no geography, class, or family-size variance. Real pedigrees are structured: reproductive skew lowers \(N_e\) further; migration raises it. Treat outputs as orders of magnitude and mechanisms, not as your personal family tree. Your documented tree, DNA segment sharing (measured in centimorgans, cM), and IBD segment analysis answer personal questions; this answers the \(2^g\) fallacy.

Glossary

  • Ahnentafel — numbered ancestor table; slot numbers double each generation (Sosa–Stradonitz numbering).
  • Pedigree collapse — reduction in distinct ancestors because relatives reproduce; measured here by \(C_g\).
  • Endogamy — marrying within a defined group (religious, geographic, caste-like) over many generations.
  • Founder effect / bottleneck — a population descends from few founders \(N_f\); drift and shared ancestry spike.
  • \(N_e\) vs \(N_c\) — effective vs census size. Unequal family sizes, sex ratio and variance cut \(N_e\) well below head-count.
  • IBD — Identity by Descent: two DNA segments are copies inherited from the same segment in a common ancestor, without recombination breaking them up in between. IBD is the gold-standard evidence of recent shared ancestry; its segment lengths (in centimorgans, cM) date the common ancestor, and the drift and coalescence mathematics in §2b/§4 is about how IBD accumulates in a small pool.
  • IBS — Identity by State: two DNA segments happen to look the same (same alleles) whether or not they came from a recent common ancestor — they may match by chance, or from ancestry so ancient it predates the pedigree. IBS \(\neq\) IBD: matching DNA is only evidence of relatedness once it's shown (or modelled) to be identical by descent, not merely identical by state.
  • Kinship \(\phi\), relationship \(r=2\phi\), inbreeding \(F\) — Wright/Malécot coefficients; \(F\) of a child = \(\phi\) of its parents.
  • Coalescence — tracing two lineages backwards until they merge in a shared ancestor.
  • MRCA (most recent common ancestor) — ambiguous term, two senses: genealogical MRCA = most recent person ancestral to two (or all) people in question (surprisingly recent, \(\sim\log_2 N\) generations); genetic MRCA = most recent ancestral DNA molecule of two gene copies at a locus (typically ancient, expected \(\approx2N_e\) generations). This site says which one it means.
  • IAP (identical ancestors point) — the generation before which everyone alive is either an ancestor of everyone alive today, or of no one.
  • Shtetl / kehillah — a small, largely Jewish town in Eastern Europe / its organised Jewish community (which kept the registers and arranged much of social life, including marriages).
  • Genealogical vs genetic ancestors — beyond ~7–10 generations, many genealogical ancestors contribute no DNA to you (no segments inherited), even though they are truly your ancestors.

Main papers

Each annotation says which part of this site's model the paper supports.

Why \(2^g\) fails: MRCA & the identical ancestors point

  • Chang, J.T. (1999). Recent common ancestors of all present-day individuals. Advances in Applied Probability 31(4):1002–1026. doi:10.1017/S0001867800009587 — two-parent Wright–Fisher model: MRCA at \(\sim\log_2 N\) generations, IAP at \(\approx1.77\log_2 N\). The theoretical backbone of the saturation point \(g^*\).
  • Rohde, D.L.T., Olson, S. & Chang, J.T. (2004). Modelling the recent common ancestry of all living humans. Nature 431(7008):562–566. doi:10.1038/nature02842 — shows recent MRCA/IAP survive population substructure and endogamy-like mating patterns, via analytic + Monte-Carlo models.

Coefficients: \(F\), kinship, coalescence, genetic ancestors

  • Wright, S. (1922). Coefficients of inbreeding and relationship. The American Naturalist 56(645):330–338. doi:10.1086/279872 — defines \(F\) and the path method used in §3.
  • Kingman, J.F.C. (1982). The coalescent. Stochastic Processes and their Applications 13(3):235–248. doi:10.1016/0304-4149(82)90011-4 — the coalescent framework behind §4 (pairwise coalescence \(\approx1/(2N_e)\) per generation).
  • Donnelly, K.P. (1983). The probability that related individuals share some section of genome identical by descent. Theoretical Population Biology 23(1):34–63. doi:10.1016/0040-5809(83)90004-7 — why genealogical ancestors beyond ~7–10 generations often contribute no DNA: the genetic-vs-genealogical ancestor distinction.

Jewish demographic history: bottlenecks, endogamy, IBD

  • Carmi, S. et al. (2014). Sequencing an Ashkenazi reference panel supports population-targeted personal genomics and illuminates Jewish and European origins. Nature Communications 5:4835. doi:10.1038/ncomms5835 — the Ashkenazi preset: effective bottleneck of \(\approx\)350 (250–420) individuals, 25–32 generations ago, then 16–53%/generation expansion. Direct source for \(N_f\) and \(g_b\).
  • Palamara, P.F., Lencz, T., Darvasi, A. & Pe'er, I. (2012). Length distributions of identity by descent reveal fine-scale demographic history. American Journal of Human Genetics 91(5):809–822. doi:10.1016/j.ajhg.2012.08.030 — derives \(N_e(g)\) trajectories from IBD segment lengths; recovers the AJ founder event between two expansions. Source for the trajectory shape in the \(N_e(g)\) chart.
  • Waldman, S. et al. (2022). Genome-wide data from medieval German Jews show that the Ashkenazi founder event pre-dated the 14th century. Cell 185(25):4703–4716.e16. doi:10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.002 — ancient DNA from 14th-century Erfurt: the founder event, high runs of homozygosity, and medieval substructure were already in place — an independent check on the bottleneck timing.
  • Xue, J., Lencz, T., Darvasi, A., Pe'er, I. & Carmi, S. (2017). The time and place of European admixture in Ashkenazi Jewish history. PLoS Genetics 13(4):e1006644. doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.1006644 — admixture \(\approx\)30 generations ago, mainly Southern European; dates the limited gene flow that the endogamy parameter \(e\) summarises.
  • Atzmon, G. et al. (2010). Abraham's children in the genome era: major Jewish diaspora populations comprise distinct genetic clusters with shared Middle Eastern ancestry. American Journal of Human Genetics 86(6):850–859. doi:10.1016/j.ajhg.2010.04.015 — IBD sharing across Ashkenazi, Sephardic and Mizrahi groups; severe AJ bottleneck + expansion; within-group relatedness \(\approx\) fifth cousins.
  • Behar, D.M. et al. (2010). The genome-wide structure of the Jewish people. Nature 466(7303):238–242. doi:10.1038/nature09103 — 14 diaspora communities vs 69 Old World populations: most Jewish groups form a tight Levant-linked cluster, Ethiopian/Indian communities cluster with neighbours — why this site refuses a single "Jewish" preset.
  • Slatkin, M. (2004). A population-genetic test of founder effects and implications for Ashkenazi Jewish diseases. American Journal of Human Genetics 75(2):282–293. doi:10.1086/423146 — founder-effect test consistent with a severe bottleneck c. 1100–1400 CE (and an earlier one c. 75 CE); the disease-allele consequences of collapse.
